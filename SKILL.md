# Plain Business English: facts at their size

## When to use

Use when writing or rewriting English business email/messages for non-native (especially Chinese L1) writers, when a draft sounds too aggressive or too vague, or when translating Chinese business correspondence intent into natural English. Not for marketing copy or legal notices.

English business register rewards calibration: a calm sentence with a number
persuades more than an excited one without. The common failures are all
inflation — enthusiasm, apology, certainty — plus padding that buries the
point. None of it is a grammar error, which is why grammar tools never catch
it; this skill does.

## Rules

1. **State facts at their size.** Match the sentence's energy to the fact's
   size: a small fact in a big sentence reads unreliable; a big fact in a
   calm sentence reads credible.
2. **Bottom line up front.** The first sentence carries the ask or the news;
   context follows. Three paragraphs of setup before the point reads as
   either hesitation or evasion.
3. **Cut inflation in all three flavors.** Enthusiasm ("genuinely excited",
   "thrilled"), apology ("sorry to bother you", "just a quick"), certainty
   ("definitely", "guaranteed") — each replaced by the concrete thing itself:
   what was done, the request, the evidence.
4. **Hedge exactly where meant.** One hedge marks genuine uncertainty; hedges
   everywhere mark a writer unsure of their own message. Where the fact is
   solid, state it plainly.
5. **Warmth comes from precision.** In first-contact and cross-cultural mail,
   a specific answer, an on-time reply, and a correct name do the warming.
   Jokes carry a real risk of misfiring across cultures and hierarchies; save
   them for relationships that already have them.
6. **Register follows the channel.** A Slack nudge and a client email are
   different garments for the same facts; pick per recipient, and when in
   doubt, one notch more formal costs nothing.

## Steps

1. **Find the point.** Read the draft and state its one point in a clause.
   Done when: the point is written down.
2. **Reorder.** Move the point into the first sentence; let support follow.
   Done when: a skimming reader gets the ask from the first two lines.
3. **De-inflate.** Replace every enthusiasm, apology, and certainty marker
   with the concrete fact or request behind it.
   Done when: zero inflation markers remain and every sentence survives as
   a statement the writer can stand behind.
4. **Calibrate hedges.** Keep one hedge per genuine uncertainty; remove the
   reflexive ones.
   Done when: each remaining hedge names something actually uncertain.
5. **Register check.** Match formality to channel and relationship; correct
   names and titles; dates and numbers in the recipient's conventions.
   Done when: the draft would look at home in the recipient's inbox, and the
   writer's own voice still sounds like the writer.

## Done when

The message leads with its point, states its facts at their size, carries no
inflation markers, hedges only real uncertainty, and matches the channel's
register — while still sounding like the person who sent it.

## Gotchas / 常见坑

- Formal is not polite: 'I would be grateful if you could...' reads heavier than 'Could you...' — match weight to the size of the ask.
- Mapping Chinese courtesy idioms literally reads sarcastic or overwrought in English; re-derive the intent, then re-express it.
- 'Please advise' and 'as per my last email' carry passive-aggressive freight in English corporate culture; restate the ask directly instead.
